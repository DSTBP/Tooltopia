## 画图

Create a publication-quality academic framework diagram for a top-tier computer science conference or journal paper.

The figure should present a complete end-to-end methodological pipeline in a single integrated canvas, combining system context, data representations, mathematical operations, algorithmic modules, intermediate results, iterative refinement, and final outputs.

Use the following visual style:

- Clean white background with a thin black rounded rectangular outer border.
- Dense but highly organized scientific layout, optimized for a research paper rather than a commercial infographic.
- Primarily left-to-right information flow, with secondary top-to-bottom branches and feedback loops where necessary.
- Divide the framework into several major methodological stages using large bold serif section titles.
- Use subtle dashed horizontal or vertical separators to distinguish algorithmic phases without creating rigid boxed subfigures.
- Maintain strong visual hierarchy: major stage titles, local process labels, mathematical expressions, matrix annotations, and minor explanatory text.
- Use publication-style serif typography similar to Times New Roman or LaTeX typesetting.
- Render mathematical variables, equations, subscripts, superscripts, Greek symbols, similarity functions, optimization objectives, thresholds, and set notation in clean LaTeX-style mathematical typography.

Use a restrained semantic color palette:

- Blue for the first data stream, query-side entities, observations, or source-domain features.
- Green for the second data stream, keyword-side entities, candidate objects, anchors, or target-domain features.
- Orange for transformations, iterative updates, reassignment, feedback arrows, and optimization transitions.
- Light gray for filtered, inactive, discarded, outdated, or secondary elements.
- Red only for warnings, rejected choices, critical thresholds, disagreement, or negative decisions.
- Use pale pastel fills for containers and medium-to-dark saturation only for selected or important elements.

Represent numerical structures using compact scientific matrices and heatmaps:

- Show co-occurrence matrices, feature matrices, similarity matrices, matching matrices, confidence matrices, or margin matrices as small rectangular grids.
- Use blue or green heatmap intensity to encode magnitude.
- Keep low-value cells white or lightly tinted.
- Highlight selected, matched, or high-confidence cells using darker saturation.
- Include representative decimal values inside selected matrices where useful.
- Keep matrix styling consistent across different pipeline stages.

Combine conceptual and mathematical visualizations:

- Use simplified research-style illustrations or flat vector icons on the input side to establish the real-world scenario, such as databases, cloud servers, clients, devices, files, users, encrypted data, networks, or historical systems.
- Gradually transition from concrete system illustrations to abstract mathematical representations toward the center of the figure.
- Use vectors, matrices, bipartite graphs, equations, ranked lists, matching pairs, confidence scores, and optimization results in the algorithmic core.
- Ensure illustrations support the scientific explanation and do not dominate the figure.

Use arrows as a semantic visual language:

- Thin blue arrows for transformations within the blue/source branch.
- Thin green arrows for transformations within the green/target branch.
- Orange arrows for major algorithmic transitions, assignment, iterative refinement, or feedback.
- Large curved orange arrows for iterative loops or information returning to a maintained pool.
- Use arrow direction consistently to reveal the computational flow at a glance.

When two related information streams are involved:

- Arrange them as visually parallel branches.
- Use symmetric structures and repeated operations to emphasize correspondence.
- Let the two branches converge at similarity computation, alignment, matching, optimization, or assignment.
- Clearly differentiate the two sides through persistent blue–green semantic coloring.

Embed equations directly beside the operations they define.

For example, place similarity equations next to similarity matrices, optimization equations next to assignment modules, margin formulas beside confidence boxes, and threshold conditions beside decision branches.

Visualize algorithmic decisions explicitly:

- Use green check marks for accepted or valid decisions.
- Use red crosses for rejected or invalid decisions.
- Use gray styling for removed candidates.
- Use highlighted green cards or nodes for retained candidates.
- Show bidirectional matching using linked paired nodes.
- Show one-way or invalid matching separately when relevant.
- Clearly distinguish selected, filtered, matched, unmatched, retained, and discarded states.

Use rounded rectangles selectively:

- Apply rounded containers only to semantically important concepts such as candidate pools, filtering rules, matching rules, confidence scores, selected results, or persistent memory components.
- Do not place every element inside a box.
- Keep borders thin and academically restrained.

For iterative or adaptive methods:

- Include a persistent pool, memory bank, anchor set, candidate set, prototype set, or trusted correspondence set as stacked cards or layered rounded panels.
- Show iterative updates entering and leaving this component with curved arrows.
- If temporal adaptation is relevant, visually compare states at time t−1 and t.
- Highlight changed correspondences using selective accent colors.
- Place convergence, agreement, stopping, or acceptance conditions close to the temporal comparison.

For ranking or selection stages:

- Show candidate scores in compact rounded boxes.
- Visually gray out removed candidates.
- Rank surviving candidates using confidence, margin, similarity, or quality scores.
- Mark the final Top-K or selected results with pale green cards.
- Preserve the visual trace from the score calculation to the final selected elements.

For the final output:

- Clearly separate application-level results from intermediate mathematical computation.
- Use a visually distinctive but still publication-compatible output region.
- Show interpretable recovered labels, matched objects, classifications, reconstructed information, predictions, or retrieved items.
- Connect the final result back to the algorithm through a clear decision arrow.

Composition requirements:

- Landscape orientation with a wide aspect ratio suitable for a two-column academic paper.
- High information density but controlled visual clutter.
- Precise alignment, balanced spacing, and consistent object sizing.
- Use repeated visual grammar across stages.
- Prioritize logical readability over decorative minimalism.
- Make the full pipeline understandable without relying heavily on the paper caption.
- Ensure that every color, arrow, container, and highlight has a consistent semantic meaning.
- Preserve sufficient whitespace around equations and major section titles.
- Avoid oversized icons, unnecessary gradients, glossy effects, 3D rendering, dramatic shadows, neon colors, cartoon aesthetics, or marketing-style graphics.

Overall aesthetic:

A rigorous, information-dense, publication-ready scientific schematic resembling a polished method overview figure from a top-tier IEEE, ACM, USENIX, NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, KDD, CCS, NDSS, or related computer science venue: precise, technical, mathematically grounded, visually hierarchical, color-consistent, and professionally typeset.
